1 Background

NoteConsulting Blueprint: Value-Driven Scope & Budget Control (Click to expand)

This case study functions as a high-fidelity HTA appraisal audit and model reconstruction portfolio, simulating the role of a Principal Health Economist or Senior Modeler in a consulting practice. It evaluates the NICE technology appraisal TA1092 concerning pembrolizumab with carboplatin and paclitaxel for first-line advanced or recurrent endometrial cancer.

In commercial HEOR consulting, project delivery is strictly constrained by billable hours and the contract’s Scope of Work (SOW). A common challenge for modelers transitioning from academia is the tendency toward academic perfectionism—spending excessive hours pursuing non-reproducible parameters or trying to replicate confidential manufacturer calculations.

This project demonstrates a pragmatic, value-driven scope management strategy under a target budget of ~15 hours. The objective is not to reproduce the confidential manufacturer workbook or match the exact confidential ICERs (which are driven by undisclosed discounts). Instead, we deliberately restrict our scope to focus on high-value technical nodes—the key structural assumptions and parameters that EAGs challenge most and to which the cost-effectiveness decisions are highly sensitive.

1.2 Clinical Context

Disease area: untreated primary advanced or recurrent endometrial cancer.

Intervention: Pembrolizumab with carboplatin and paclitaxel, followed by pembrolizumab maintenance (up to 14 additional cycles).

Comparator: Carboplatin with paclitaxel followed by placebo maintenance in the clinical trial setting (KEYNOTE-868), and carboplatin with paclitaxel alone without maintenance in the economic model setting representing standard NHS clinical practice.

Key clinical trial: KEYNOTE-868 / NRG-GY018.

Main modelling issue: the intervention combines immunotherapy with platinum-taxane chemotherapy. The health economic question is whether the additional cost of pembrolizumab is justified by improved progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), especially when long-term OS data are immature.

NoteModeler’s Initial Risk Screening & HTA Audit Focus (Click to expand)

When evaluating a manufacturer’s cost-effectiveness submission for an oncology immunotherapy combination, an experienced HTA model auditor screens for standard systemic risk nodes. In this appraisal, our initial risk-screening checklist targets four critical domains:

  1. Survival Extrapolation & Tail Plausibility (High Risk):
    • Audit Target: When clinical trial follow-up is relatively short (median < 2 years), the choice of parametric models is highly sensitive. We must verify if the long-term overall survival (OS) tails are clinically plausible or if they project unrealistic lifetime “immortal patient” fractions.
    • Key Focus: Subgroup sample sizes (dMMR vs. pMMR) and the risk of PFS and OS curves crossing in the long term, which violates logical consistency.
  2. Immunotherapy Treatment Duration & Efficacy Waning (High Risk):
    • Audit Target: Immunotherapies like pembrolizumab are typically capped (e.g., a 2-year stopping rule). The model must not assume that treatment benefit persists indefinitely after treatment discontinuation.
    • Key Focus: Implementing and testing treatment-effect waning scenarios (e.g., waning starting at 2 or 5 years and converging to control hazards) to evaluate the stability of the ICER.
  3. Subgroup Applicability & Data Maturity (Medium Risk):
    • Audit Target: The clinical and economic benefits may differ significantly between mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) and proficient (pMMR) cohorts. We need to check if separate models are statistically stable and if baseline characteristics align with the UK target population.
  4. Resource Cost & Subsequent Therapy Alignments (Medium Risk):
    • Audit Target: Subsequent treatment mixes in the comparator arm must reflect real-world NHS standard pathways rather than non-reimbursed trial settings, as differences in active subsequent therapies can distort the cost delta.

2 EAG Critique Lens

2.1 How the EAG Reviews This Type of Case

The EAG is not only checking whether the model runs. It is asking whether the company’s assumptions are:

  • internally consistent with trial evidence;
  • externally plausible against clinical knowledge;
  • aligned with NHS treatment pathways;
  • transparent enough for committee decision-making;
  • conservative enough when evidence is immature.

This distinction is important for the portfolio: a health economist in consulting must be able to understand both the company logic and the EAG logic.

2.2 Working Reflection on Issue 1

Issue 1 is my first example of how the EAG examines the alignment between trial design, trial objectives, trial results, and later interpretation of those results.

My current understanding is that the EAG is concerned about target consistency. If the trial was originally designed to answer one type of question, for example treatment effects within separate dMMR and pMMR cohorts, but the later economic modelling relies on a different question, for example an all-comer analysis combining those populations, then there is a risk of misalignment between the evidence generation process and the modelled decision problem.

In practical HTA terms, the concern is not simply that all-comer analysis is wrong. The concern is that a post-hoc all-comer analysis may be less reliable if it was not the original basis of the trial design and statistical analysis plan. This can create uncertainty about whether the estimated treatment effect is truly representative of the population used in the economic model.

This issue is therefore mainly an evidence validity issue rather than a direct cost-effectiveness parameter issue. It affects confidence in the survival inputs and subgroup assumptions that feed into the model. For this portfolio, I will treat Issue 1 as part of the EAG’s broader logic: before challenging model extrapolations, the EAG first checks whether the clinical evidence being extrapolated is aligned with the decision problem.

Issue 2 extends the same evidence-validity concern to more granular subgroups. The EAG notes that some NICE-specified subgroups, such as local versus metastatic recurrence or prior surgery history, were not systematically captured in the pivotal trial. My takeaway is that this is not a main technical replication target, but it reinforces an important appraisal principle: broad modelled indications can become uncertain when the trial evidence does not fully describe clinically relevant heterogeneity.

This type of subgroup limitation is probably common in oncology appraisals because pivotal trials are rarely large enough to support robust analyses across many clinically detailed subgroups. The key point is not that every missing subgroup analysis is equally important. The EAG appears to elevate subgroup limitations when they are decision-relevant: when the subgroup was specified in the NICE scope, may have different prognosis or treatment effect, affects the clinical pathway, or could change the cost-effectiveness conclusion.

2.3 Working Reflection on Issue 3

Issue 3 is a stronger candidate for a technical deep dive because it connects directly to survival modelling. The EAG’s concern is that KEYNOTE-868 has relatively short follow-up, while the economic model needs to project PFS, OS, costs, and QALYs over a much longer horizon.

My current interpretation is that short follow-up is not a problem simply because “more data would always be better”. It becomes decision-relevant when the observed trial period covers only the early part of the survival curves, but a material share of the modelled incremental benefit is generated in the extrapolated tail. In that setting, the model result depends heavily on assumptions about curve selection, long-term tail behaviour, treatment effect duration, and clinical plausibility.

After checking the EAG report and later factual-accuracy material in the committee papers, my more precise working interpretation is that the short-follow-up concern can be condensed into a few case-specific signals rather than a universal threshold. The median follow-up appears to be around 16.3 months, while the planned active treatment period is approximately 2.2 years. This means that the median observation time does not even fully cover the planned treatment duration. In addition, the pembrolizumab arm median OS was not reached, meaning the trial had not yet observed the time point at which 50% of patients had died.

These are not formal NICE cut-offs, but they are useful data-maturity signals. If median OS has not been reached, long-term OS benefit is still not directly observed. If median follow-up is shorter than the intended treatment duration, there is limited evidence about what happens after patients complete or stop pembrolizumab. This makes assumptions about sustained treatment effect, treatment waning, and long-term survival tails especially important.

NotebookLM review of the EAG material suggests that the EAG’s concern was broader than these two signals. The EAG also appears to use several related judgement signals when assessing whether the follow-up is sufficient for long-term extrapolation:

This gives a more defensible interpretation than using a simple follow-up-to-horizon ratio. The EAG’s concern is cumulative: immature OS, sparse KM tails, divergent extrapolation models, and uncertain post-treatment benefit all point in the same direction. Together, they make the long-term extrapolated survival benefit less secure.

For the portfolio, I should therefore avoid treating Issue 3 as a generic limitation. The useful demonstration is to show the gap between observed evidence and modelled horizon. A practical way to do this is:

  • show the reconstructed KM curve and fitted parametric survival curve on the same plot;
  • mark the approximate observed follow-up boundary with a vertical line;
  • shade the extrapolated region beyond the observed trial data;
  • compare plausible parametric distributions that fit similarly during the observed period but diverge in the long-term tail;
  • report landmark survival predictions, for example 5-year, 10-year, and 20-year PFS/OS.

This also clarifies the role of uncertainty. Short follow-up can widen statistical uncertainty, but the larger HTA concern is often structural uncertainty: different plausible extrapolation models may produce meaningfully different long-term survival, even if their in-sample AIC/BIC or visual fit is similar. This is why an EAG may challenge an AIC-selected curve if its long-term behaviour lacks clinical credibility.

2.4 Working Reflection on Issue 4

Issue 4 is a utility evidence limitation. The EAG notes that HRQoL/patient-reported outcomes were assessed in the pMMR cohort only, with no equivalent dMMR HRQoL data available. The company’s explanation is that the smaller dMMR sample size created insufficient statistical power, so HRQoL analyses were prespecified for pMMR.

My takeaway is that this is not a main technical replication target at this stage, but it is important for the appraisal logic. In a cost-utility model, HRQoL evidence feeds into health-state utilities and therefore QALYs. If utility evidence comes only from pMMR patients but is applied more broadly, then the representativeness of utility inputs becomes uncertain, especially for subgroup-specific cost-effectiveness.

4.1.3 Safe Fitting Wrapper Function

Some distributions can fail for sparse or immature OS data. This function catches failures and returns a row explaining what happened.

Code 
`%||%` <- function(x, y) if (is.null(x)) y else x

fit_one_distribution <- function(dat, dist_label, dist_name) {
  tryCatch(
    {
      fit <- flexsurvreg(Surv(time_months, status) ~ 1, data = dat, dist = dist_name)
      n_par <- nrow(fit$res)
      fit_aic <- as.numeric(fit$AIC %||% NA_real_)
      fit_loglik <- as.numeric(fit$loglik %||% NA_real_)

      tibble(
        dist_label = dist_label,
        dist_name = dist_name,
        fit = list(fit),
        converged = TRUE,
        error = NA_character_,
        aic = fit_aic,
        bic = -2 * fit_loglik + log(nrow(dat)) * n_par,
        loglik = fit_loglik,
        n = nrow(dat),
        events = sum(dat$status)
      )
    },
    error = function(e) {
      tibble(
        dist_label = dist_label,
        dist_name = dist_name,
        fit = list(NULL),
        converged = FALSE,
        error = conditionMessage(e),
        aic = NA_real_,
        bic = NA_real_,
        loglik = NA_real_,
        n = nrow(dat),
        events = sum(dat$status)
      )
    }
  )
}

4.1.4 Details of the Wrapper Logic

This function is a small wrapper around flexsurvreg(). The reason to write a wrapper is that we want to fit many distributions across many curves. Some fits may fail, especially for immature OS curves with few events. If one fit fails, we do not want the whole document to stop.

The function has three inputs:

fit_one_distribution(dat, dist_label, dist_name)
  • dat: the pseudo-IPD for one curve, for example pmmr_pfs_pembro_ipd.
  • dist_label: the readable name used in output tables, for example "weibull".
  • dist_name: the name required by flexsurvreg(), for example "weibull" or "llogis".

The helper:

`%||%` <- function(x, y) if (is.null(x)) y else x

means: if x is missing or NULL, use y instead. Here it prevents the code from breaking if a fitted object does not contain AIC or loglik.

The core model fit is:

fit <- flexsurvreg(Surv(time_months, status) ~ 1, data = dat, dist = dist_name)

This means:

  • use time_months and status from dat;
  • fit one survival curve, because the right-hand side is ~ 1;
  • use the parametric distribution named in dist_name.

The object fit is a fitted survival model. It contains parameter estimates, log-likelihood, AIC, and other information. We store it inside a tibble using:

fit = list(fit)

This creates a list-column. Without list(), a complex model object cannot fit cleanly inside one table cell.

The successful-output part returns one row:

tibble(
  dist_label = dist_label,
  dist_name = dist_name,
  fit = list(fit),
  converged = TRUE,
  error = NA_character_,
  aic = fit_aic,
  bic = -2 * fit_loglik + log(nrow(dat)) * n_par,
  loglik = fit_loglik,
  n = nrow(dat),
  events = sum(dat$status)
)

Key fields:

  • converged = TRUE: the model fitted successfully.
  • error = NA_character_: there was no error message.
  • aic: model fit criterion reported by flexsurv.
  • bic: calculated manually using -2 * logLik + log(n) * number_of_parameters.
  • n: number of pseudo-patients in this curve.
  • events: number of observed events.

The tryCatch() part is the safety mechanism:

tryCatch(
  { successful fitting code },
  error = function(e) { failure output }
)

If flexsurvreg() fails, the function still returns a row. That row has:

  • fit = list(NULL)
  • converged = FALSE
  • error = conditionMessage(e)
  • aic, bic, loglik as NA

This is useful for modelling workflow QA. Instead of silently losing failed fits, the output table records exactly which distribution failed and why.

In HEOR work, this matters because sparse OS data can make complex models unstable. A reviewer or EAG would want to know not only which model had the best AIC, but also whether some candidate models failed, behaved oddly, or were unsuitable for extrapolation.

5.2 EAG Issue 3 Framework & Extrapolation-Zone Plotting

5.2.1 Introduction to Issue 3 Demonstration

The EAG’s Issue 3 is not just saying that longer follow-up would be preferable. The practical concern is that KEYNOTE-868 provides relatively early survival evidence, while the economic model requires long-term PFS and OS predictions.

In the EAG report, several values are redacted in the Issue 3 table. Later consultation comments in the same committee papers refer to the median follow-up as 16.3 months. I use that value below as a working interpretation, while noting that the value is redacted in the Issue 3 table itself.

This section uses pMMR OS, pembro arm as the worked example. OS is usually one of the most important drivers of life years and QALYs in oncology models, and short follow-up is especially important when OS benefit is immature.

5.3.3 Appraisal Interpretation

This section turns the EAG’s Issue 3 into a reproducible modelling demonstration:

  • the EAG’s concern is translated into concrete modelling checks;
  • the observed follow-up, 2-year boundary, and 20-year horizon are shown on the same scale;
  • alternative distributions show the range of plausible long-term tails;
  • landmark predictions quantify how much the tails differ;
  • the interpretation links directly to NICE/EAG judgement about survival extrapolation uncertainty.

The key portfolio message is: short follow-up matters when the cost-effectiveness result depends on survival benefits generated beyond the observed trial period. The issue is not that extrapolation is avoidable; oncology economic models often require extrapolation. The EAG concern is that, with immature OS and limited follow-up, the long-term survival benefit may be driven more by modelling assumptions than by observed trial evidence.

      ) * discount,

fit_one_spline <- function(dat, k, scale, label) {
  tryCatch({
    fit     <- flexsurvspline(Surv(time_months, status) ~ 1, data = dat, k = k, scale = scale)
    fit_aic <- as.numeric(AIC(fit) %||% NA_real_)
    fit_ll  <- as.numeric(fit$loglik %||% NA_real_)
    tibble(
      dist_label = label,
      fit        = list(fit),
      converged  = TRUE,
      error      = NA_character_,
      aic        = fit_aic,
      loglik     = fit_ll,
      n          = nrow(dat),
      events     = sum(dat$status)
    )
  }, error = function(e) {
    tibble(
      dist_label = label,
      fit        = list(NULL),
      converged  = FALSE,
      error      = conditionMessage(e),
      aic        = NA_real_,
      loglik     = NA_real_,
      n          = nrow(dat),
      events     = sum(dat$status)
    )
  })
}

get_spline_surv <- function(fit_obj, times) {
  # Unwrap list(model_obj) wrapper only if not already a flexsurv model
  if (!inherits(fit_obj, "flexsurvreg")) fit_obj <- fit_obj[[1]]
  s <- summary(fit_obj, t = times, type = "survival", ci = FALSE)
  if (is.data.frame(s))                    return(as.numeric(s$est))
  if (is.list(s) && is.data.frame(s[[1]])) return(as.numeric(s[[1]]$est))
  if (is.list(s) && is.numeric(s[[1]]))    return(as.numeric(s[[1]]))
  stop(paste("Cannot extract survival: class(s)=", class(s), "class(s[[1]])=", class(s[[1]])))
}

apply_os_tail_reduction <- function(dat, start_week = 104, end_week = 520, final_fraction = 0.5) {
  dat |>
    select(arm, time_weeks, time_months, PFS, OS) |>
    tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = arm, values_from = c(PFS, OS)) |>
    mutate(
      reduction_fraction = case_when(
        time_weeks <= start_week ~ 1,
        time_weeks >= end_week ~ final_fraction,
        TRUE ~ 1 - (1 - final_fraction) * (time_weeks - start_week) / (end_week - start_week)

      PD = pmax(OS - PFS, 0),

8.4 Clinical Logic Validation and Capping under Parameter Uncertainty

While the EAG-preferred spline models for the pMMR cohort (Table 2) offer superior statistical fit during the trial period, they introduce a clinical logic violation: the long-term extrapolated PFS curve for the placebo + CT arm (2-knot hazards) can cross and exceed the pembrolizumab + CT arm (1-knot hazards) in the tail.

During the appraisal consultations, the company pointed out this logical inconsistency, arguing that it is clinically implausible for the control arm to have superior PFS at any point given the higher response rate and observed treatment effect in the pembrolizumab arm. The NHS England Cancer Drugs Fund clinical lead supported this view. Although the EAG defended the crossing as an artifact of long-term extrapolation uncertainty that did not invalidate the observed period fit, the NICE Appraisal Committee ultimately mandated that the placebo PFS curve be logically capped at the pembrolizumab curve.

In our reconstructed pseudo-IPD deterministic base case, the point-estimate Placebo PFS and Pembro PFS curves remain extremely close in the tail but do not cross. The cap is therefore inactive in the base case (crossover week = NA), yielding identical cost-effectiveness results for capped and uncapped runs.

However, in HEOR modeling, cost-effectiveness must be evaluated under parameter uncertainty. During Probabilistic Sensitivity Analysis (PSA), we propagate the statistical estimation error of the fitted survival curves. Instead of varying a single parameter by a arbitrary range, we vary the joint regression parameters (spline coefficients) of the PFS models for both treatment arms:

  • Pembrolizumab + CT PFS: The 3 spline coefficients (gamma0, gamma1, gamma2) of the 1-knot hazard spline model.
  • Placebo + CT PFS: The 4 spline coefficients (gamma0, gamma1, gamma2, gamma3) of the 2-knot hazard spline model.

The variation range of these parameters is defined by their joint multivariate normal distribution (MVN): \[\boldsymbol{\gamma} \sim \mathcal{MVN}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\] where \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\) represents the vector of estimated coefficients (the point estimates or means) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is the estimated variance-covariance matrix (VCOV) of the coefficients from the regression model. This approach propagates both the fitting uncertainty of each parameter and their mathematical correlation. Due to the tail sensitivity of flexible spline models, these parameter draws frequently cause the Placebo PFS curve to cross and exceed the Pembro PFS curve. Implementing the logical cap is a critical structural model guardrail to ensure that every simulated PSA iteration remains clinically valid.

To demonstrate how this guardrail behaves under joint parameter uncertainty, we implement a mini-PSA loop (100 iterations) sampling the spline parameter vectors from their respective MVN distributions, and evaluate the frequency of crossovers and the cost-effectiveness impact of capping:

  # Check if Placebo PFS > Pembro PFS at any point
  crossovers[b] <- any(pfs_placebo_b > pfs_pembro_b)
  
  # Build psm_data for uncapped
  psm_uncapped_b <- tibble(
    arm = rep(c("Pembro + chemo", "Placebo + chemo"), each = length(ce_time_grid$time_weeks)),
    time_weeks = rep(ce_time_grid$time_weeks, 2),
    time_months = rep(ce_time_grid$time_months, 2),
    PFS = c(pfs_pembro_b, pfs_placebo_b),
    OS = c(os_pembro_det, os_placebo_det)
  ) |>
    mutate(
      PFS  = pmin(PFS, OS),
      PF   = PFS,
      PD   = pmax(OS - PFS, 0),
      Dead = pmax(1 - OS, 0)
    )
  
  # Build psm_data for capped
  pfs_placebo_capped_b <- pmin(pfs_placebo_b, pfs_pembro_b)
  psm_capped_b <- tibble(
    arm = rep(c("Pembro + chemo", "Placebo + chemo"), each = length(ce_time_grid$time_weeks)),
    time_weeks = rep(ce_time_grid$time_weeks, 2),
    time_months = rep(ce_time_grid$time_months, 2),
    PFS = c(pfs_pembro_b, pfs_placebo_capped_b),
    OS = c(os_pembro_det, os_placebo_det)
  ) |>
    mutate(
      PFS  = pmin(PFS, OS),
      PF   = PFS,
      PD   = pmax(OS - PFS, 0),
      Dead = pmax(1 - OS, 0)
    )
  
  results_psa_uncapped[[b]] <- run_ce_trace(psm_uncapped_b)
  results_psa_capped[[b]]   <- run_ce_trace(psm_capped_b)
}

8.5 HTA Decision Interpretation & Consulting Takeaways

In HEOR modeling, the choice of survival models is rarely a purely statistical exercise. The “crossover and capping” debate in the pembrolizumab appraisal teaches several critical consulting lessons regarding the thinking process of HTA bodies:

  1. Primacy of Clinical Logic Over Statistical Fit: Spline models (like the Royston-Parmar flexible parametric models) offer excellent empirical fit to the trial period due to their flexible knots. However, they lack structural constraints. When statistical optimization yields clinical absurdities—such as a placebo arm outperforming an active immunotherapy arm in the long term—clinical plausibility must serve as a hard boundary condition to restrict statistical extrapolation.
  2. Methodological Pragmatism (The Capping Solution): Instead of discarding the spline models entirely (which would require selecting a less fitting parametric distribution in the trial period), the NICE committee accepted the EAG’s splines but mandated a post-hoc logical cap. This shows that HTA bodies favor pragmatic structural adjustments (capping or waning) that preserve the integrity of the trial period fit while ensuring safe tail behavior.
  3. The Role of Stakeholder Mediation: The HTA process is a negotiation. While the EAG initially defended the curve crossing as a negligible mathematical artifact of uncertainty, the company and the Cancer Drugs Fund clinical lead successfully argued that clinical implausibility creates decision uncertainty. The committee’s role is to mediate by validating the technical fit of the EAG while enforcing the clinical reality of the clinical lead.
  4. Subgroup-Specific Tail Plausibility: For the dMMR cohort, the small sample size made complex splines or piecewise models unstable and prone to optimistic bias, leading the EAG to prefer simple parametric models. For the larger pMMR cohort, splines were justified to avoid the company’s overly optimistic log-logistic tail, but they required logical capping.
  5. The Divergence Between Model Validity and ICER Impact: As shown in the probabilistic analysis, the difference in the mean ICER between the capped and uncapped spline models is extremely small (around £8, or ~0.01%). This is because the crossover occurs in the long-term tail where PFS probability in both arms is already very close to zero, and the difference is further minimized by the 3.5% annual discount rate. However, in HTA appraisals, logical corrections are driven by structural validity and scientific credibility, not just by their numerical impact on the ICER. A model that allows placebo patients to have better progression-free survival than active treatment patients is structurally flawed, compromising the credibility of the entire submission. HTA committees will reject model structures that allow clinically impossible states, regardless of whether the numerical impact is £8 or £8,000. Therefore, implementing guardrails is a matter of professional model quality assurance.
  6. Strategic Alignment of Clinical Logic and Commercial Interests: The crossover debate illustrates how clinical critique and commercial submission strategy can align. While the company framed its objection to the crossing curves on clinical and logical grounds, implementing the logical cap mathematically favored the cost-effectiveness case for pembrolizumab. Capping the Placebo PFS at the Pembro PFS curve reduces the Placebo PFS probability in the tail, which shifts simulated patient-time from the progression-free (PF) state to the progressed disease (PD) state. Because the PF health state is assigned a higher utility (0.814) than the PD state (0.720), this shift lowers the cumulative QALYs in the Placebo arm. This increases the incremental QALY benefit (\(\Delta E\)) in favor of the active treatment, which mathematically reduces the ICER. In HTA consulting, identifying areas where clinical realism arguments also happen to strengthen the cost-effectiveness case is a key element of strategic value demonstration.
