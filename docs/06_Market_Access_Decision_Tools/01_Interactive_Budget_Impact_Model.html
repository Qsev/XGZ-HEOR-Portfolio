Interactive Budget Impact Model

Venetoclax in acute myeloid leukaemia — a transparent, source-traced rebuild

Author

Xiaoge Zhang, PhD

Published

August 22, 2026

ImportantSource and status

Every parameter in this tool comes from one published, openly licensed study:

Palacios A, Espinola N, Gonzalez JM, Rojas-Roque C, Rivas MM, Kanevski D, Morisset P, Augustovski F, Pichon-Riviere A, Bardach A. Budget impact analysis of venetoclax for the management of acute myeloid leukemia from the perspective of the social security and the private sector in Argentina. PLoS One. 2024;19(1):e0295798. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0295798 — CC-BY 4.0.

This is a methods demonstration, not a client deliverable, not a validated reimbursement model, and not a pricing recommendation. It contains no confidential or commercial data. Market shares in the source are projections supplied by the manufacturer and validated by a Delphi panel of six onco-haematologists; they are not observed market data.

Every input carries a source tag — a table number, a stated derivation, or an explicit assumption. There are three assumptions and they are labelled as such.

What a budget impact model actually is

A cost-effectiveness model asks whether a therapy is worth its price. A budget impact model asks a narrower and more immediate question: can this payer afford to adopt it, and when does the money move?

The answer is a subtraction:

the world with the new therapy, minus the world without it

That subtraction is the whole model. It is also where most of the difficulty sits, because the two worlds must be built on the same population, the same time horizon and the same cost categories, and only then differenced. Without an explicit market share layer there is only one world and no budget impact at all — which is why that layer, not the headline number, is the part worth inspecting first.

The rebuild

The tool below reconstructs the published model from its parameters rather than restating its results. Seven layers, in the order a reviewer would work through them:

  1. the epidemiology funnel, from covered lives to treatable patients
  2. market share in both worlds, year by year — the layer that makes it a budget impact model
  3. cost per patient per year, in six components, built bottom-up
  4. budget impact, annual and cumulative, with per-member-per-month
  5. one-way sensitivity analysis, against the published tornado
  6. reconciliation with the published results, including where it does not agree
  7. provenance for every parameter

Three things surfaced during the rebuild that are worth naming, because they are the parts a spreadsheet would hide:

  • The published supplement contradicts itself. S1 Table and S7 Table assign the venetoclax + decitabine and venetoclax + LDAC market shares in opposite order. Reconciling each against the drug costs in Table 4 settles it, and the model follows the assignment that reproduces the published spend.
  • Drug cost had to be rebuilt as a function of treatment duration, not taken as a fixed annual total. Duration is the leading sensitivity driver in the source; with a frozen annual cost it cannot move the result at all, and the sensitivity analysis would be theatre.
  • Calibrating to force agreement made the answer worse. Correcting each cost component to match the published levels distorts the difference between the two worlds — which is the only quantity a budget impact model reports. The rebuild is left uncalibrated and the residual is shown.

Technical notes

The tool is a static page: three JavaScript files, no server, no build step, no dependencies. The separation is deliberate and is part of the point.

  • bim_params.js — every parameter, each with a source tag. No logic.
  • bim_engine.js — pure calculation. No DOM, no globals, parameters passed in, so the sensitivity analysis perturbs a copy rather than the base case.
  • bim_ui.js — rendering and event binding only.

A reviewer who wants to audit the model does not need to read the interface code. They open the parameter file and check it against the paper — which is the same thing they would ask to do with a spreadsheet, and rarely can.

Limitations

Three years, 28-day cycles, deterministic, no probabilistic sensitivity analysis — matching the source. No QALYs or ICERs: this is a budget impact model, and mixing the two questions is a common way to answer neither. Costs are US dollars at 2020 prices with no inflation adjustment, following the source’s stated reason for excluding it. Administration and hospitalisation do not fully reconcile to the published figures because the unit costs they depend on are not reported; the gap is quantified in the reconciliation panel rather than closed by fitting.
